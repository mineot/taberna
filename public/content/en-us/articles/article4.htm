Travel notebook · 2 days

A weekend in Pedra Clara

Stone paths, slowly brewed coffee, and a mountain town that seems to follow a different clock.

A fictional travel account by Marina Reis.

The road began to climb just beyond the last coffee plantation. My phone lost its signal, mist settled between the trees, and the bus driver announced Pedra Clara as if he were revealing a secret. I stepped off in the main square shortly before nine, carrying only a backpack and a short list of places that might not even exist.

Saturday — Walking without a shortcut

You can cross the town in twenty minutes, but it took me all morning. The houses have colorful doors, narrow gardens, and hand-painted signs. On Market Street, I found a bakery with four tables, a stopped clock, and cornmeal cakes still warm from the oven. I ordered one, then another, and heard from the woman behind the counter that no one there counts cakes or time.

I continued to the old railway station, now transformed into a library. Bookshelves occupy the place where waiting benches once stood. In a box near the entrance, residents leave notes recommending books. I chose a collection of short stories and wrote a recommendation before I had even finished it, trusting the excitement of the first page.

In the late afternoon, I climbed the Hydrangea Path. The trail is short, but the stones become slippery after the rain. From the lookout, the rooftops seemed like pieces fitted between the forest and the mountains. I stayed until the lights in the square came on one by one.

Notes from the first day

Wear comfortable shoes: the hills are part of the experience.

Try the cornmeal cake with filtered coffee.

Set aside time for the library at the station.

Sunday — The sound of the square

I woke to bells and a conversation outside the guesthouse window. On Sundays, producers from the region set up stalls in the square. There were cheeses, preserves, baskets, herbs, and a man who repaired old radios while telling stories about each one.

I bought a small jar of honey and sat near the bandstand. A group was tuning instruments for the noon performance. Children ran between the stalls, vendors called customers by name, and no one seemed concerned with turning Sunday into anything other than Sunday.

Before leaving, I returned to the bakery. The woman behind the counter recognized my backpack and wrapped two cornmeal cakes for the journey. The bus descended the mountain in the early afternoon, after the mist had already retreated. Pedra Clara disappeared beyond the first bend, but I carried with me the feeling that some places do not ask for a list of attractions. They ask for presence.
