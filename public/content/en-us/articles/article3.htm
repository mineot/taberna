Chronicle · By Daniel Avelar

The last table on the sidewalk

Some places look empty only to those who do not yet know their stories.

Café Avenida had six tables on the sidewalk, but served only five. The last one, against the blue wall, remained empty even on Saturdays when the line reached the flower stand. There was no reserved sign or broken chair. Even so, no one sat there.

New customers would ask about it. Sebastião, the waiter, replied that the table was waiting for someone. He said this while straightening the sugar bowl and wiping a tabletop that never seemed dusty. If they insisted on knowing who, he pointed to the menu and recommended the orange cake.

For many years, the table had belonged, by habit rather than contract, to a woman named Irene. She arrived at ten past three, ordered filtered coffee, and opened a red notebook. She wrote until the church bell struck four. Then she put away the notebook, left two coins beneath the cup, and continued down the avenue.

No one knew exactly what Irene wrote. The baker said they were letters. The woman at the flower stand swore she had seen drawings of dresses. Sebastião, who brought her coffee every day, never tried to read it. For him, it was enough to know that while Irene wrote, the table stopped being a piece of furniture and became a kind of address.

Some people inhabit a place so completely that they keep arriving even after they stop appearing.

One day, Irene did not come. Sebastião served other coffees, cleared other cups, and looked at the clock at ten past three. The following week, he left the table free by accident. Then he did it out of hope. By the time he noticed, the gesture had already become a tradition, and traditions are old questions no one expects to answer anymore.

On a windy Tuesday, a young woman stopped beside the table. She carried a crumpled envelope and had the same way of studying the storefront before entering. She asked whether she could sit there. Sebastião hesitated, but the sidewalk was empty. He placed a cup in front of her and, without thinking, brought a slice of orange cake as well.

The young woman opened the envelope. From inside, she took a red notebook, worn around the edges. She ran a hand over its cover and smiled like someone recognizing a landscape. She said her grandmother had written many stories at that table and that she wanted to see the place where all of them began.

Sebastião did not ask what was on the pages. He simply straightened the sugar bowl, removed the menu stand, and went back inside the café. Since that day, the sixth table has been served again. It remains reserved, but now for anyone who arrives carrying a story that still has no address.
